PATHOGENICITY

Pathogenicity

The pathogenesis of Legionnaires’ disease involves bacterial entry into alveolar macrophages, intracellular survival, immune evasion and pulmonary tissue injury.

Introduction & Transmission

Legionella pneumophila is a Gram-negative bacterium responsible for Legionnaires’ disease, a severe form of pneumonia in humans. It is naturally found in freshwater environments, where it survives and multiplies inside free-living amoebae (Romanov and O’Connor, 2024). Human infection occurs mainly through inhalation of contaminated water aerosols produced by sources such as cooling towers, air-conditioning systems and showers (Iliadi et al., 2022). After inhalation, the bacteria reach the lower respiratory tract and enter the alveoli of the lungs. There, they encounter alveolar macrophages, which are immune cells responsible for engulfing and destroying microorganisms. However, L. pneumophila has developed specialised mechanisms that allow it to survive and multiply inside these immune cells, avoiding destruction by the host's immune system (Mondino et al., 2020).

Adherence and Cellular Invasion

The infection process begins when L. pneumophila attaches to the surface of host cells, particularly alveolar macrophages. According to Iliadi et al. (2022), surface-associated proteins, including the major outer membrane protein (MOMP) and the macrophage infectivity potentiator (Mip) protein, play important roles in bacterial interactions with host cells and the development of infection. Bacterial surface structures also contribute to the initial attachment. Following adherence, the macrophage takes up the bacterium through phagocytosis, enclosing it within a membrane-bound compartment called a phagosome. One described mechanism of bacterial uptake is known as coiling phagocytosis, during which the host cell membrane wraps around the bacterium through an unusual coiling movement (Mondino et al., 2020). Once internalised, L. pneumophila begins to interfere with the normal intracellular processes of the macrophage, preparing to establish an environment that supports its survival and replication (Romanov and O’Connor, 2024).

Intracellular Survival & Immune Evasion

After entering the macrophage, L. pneumophila must avoid being destroyed by the host cell's defence mechanisms. Its most important virulence mechanism is the Dot/Icm type IV secretion system (T4SS), which acts as a molecular delivery system, injecting bacterial effector proteins directly into the host cell (Mondino et al., 2020; Romanov and O’Connor, 2024). These effector proteins manipulate host cellular processes and prevent the phagosome from following the normal pathway towards fusion with lysosomes, where bacteria would normally be destroyed. Instead, the bacterium modifies the surrounding membrane and recruits vesicles from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), creating a specialised protective compartment known as the Legionella-containing vacuole (LCV) (Romanov and O’Connor, 2024). Furthermore, Xie et al. (2023) explain that the SidE family of effector proteins performs a specialised ubiquitination reaction, modifying host proteins and interfering with normal cellular functions. This allows L. pneumophila to manipulate its intracellular environment, evade important immune defence mechanisms and establish conditions suitable for bacterial survival and replication (Xie et al., 2023; Mondino et al., 2020).

Bacterial Replication & Tissue Damage

Once the LCV has been established, L. pneumophila begins to multiply inside this specialised compartment, using nutrients obtained from the host cell to support bacterial growth (Romanov and O’Connor, 2024). As replication progresses, the number of bacteria within the LCV increases, eventually reaching high numbers. When conditions become less favourable, including nutrient limitation, the bacteria undergo changes associated with their transmissive phase, preparing to leave the host cell and infect neighbouring cells (Mondino et al., 2020). During this stage, bacterial virulence factors, including secreted enzymes such as metalloproteases, contribute to host-cell damage (Iliadi et al., 2022). The infected macrophage can subsequently undergo cell death and lysis, releasing bacteria into the surrounding environment. The released bacteria can then infect other host cells, continuing the infection cycle (Romanov and O’Connor, 2024). The destruction of host cells, together with the inflammatory response triggered by the infection, contributes to lung tissue damage and the development of pneumonia characteristic of Legionnaires’ disease (Mondino et al., 2020; Iliadi et al., 2022).

Intracellular pathogenesis and host range

Legionella is an intracellular vacuolar pathogen of protozoa and humans. Legionella replicates within protozoa, particularly free-living amoeba in the environment. Intracellular replication requires a Type IVB secretion system that translocates effector proteins into the host cell where they function to modulate host cellular processes. Legionella evolution, often through the acquisition of foreign genetic material, has enabled expansion of its host range, including its ability to transition from environmental reservoirs to humans. Legionella is able to replicate within alveolar macrophages causing respiratory illness in humans; however, disease severity varies depending on immune system competency.
